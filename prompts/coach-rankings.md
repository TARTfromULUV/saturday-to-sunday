# Ranking the coaches

An evergreen piece for a quiet week about the coaching profession — and NOT
the same piece every time. The treatment in your brief decides which story
this is; follow it, because the last issue told a different one.

This is explicitly NOT a ranking of the best teams. Whatever the treatment,
the lens is the job being done, not the roster doing it.

700 to 1,000 words.

Two lists, two halves of the profession:

- `candidates` — the coaches getting the most out of what they have: record,
  conference record and standing, `againstExpectation` (where the team sits
  against where it was picked, in words — the preseason number itself is not
  given and never cited), national offensive and defensive ranks, tenure,
  streaks, `career` (his record and titles across every job), and
  `programStanding`, the same program rank the strugglers carry.
- `strugglers` — the other end: coaches at BIG jobs (`programStanding` ranks
  every program by its own published history — titles, weeks ranked, playoff
  trips) whose record is under water. These are the men winning least with
  the most, and the treatments about underachievement and program futures are
  written from this list. If it is empty, the sport's big jobs are all fine
  this year — say so in a line if the treatment needs it, and work from
  `candidates` instead.

## Structure

- Open by stating the criteria in one paragraph, and be honest that this is a
  different question from who has the best team.
- Rank the top ten to fifteen, numbered. Each gets the record and two or three
  sentences of argument. `againstExpectation` is your strongest evidence, and
  `program.recentSeasons` is the second: a 9-2 at a school that was 4-8 for
  three years is a different achievement from a 9-2 at one that is always 9-2.
- Give real weight to coaches at programs with no history of this, and to
  anyone whose defense or offense ranks far better than the roster should
  allow.
- Include a short section on two or three big names from `strugglers` — the
  coaches at the biggest jobs whose season does not belong on this list — and
  say why not. That section is what makes it worth reading. Nobody outside
  the two lists is named.

## Rules

- Never cite a rating, a prestige value, or any number from a game menu.
- Record, rankings, expectation gap, and national statistical ranks only.
- Do not rank a coach on reputation. Rank the season in front of you.
- No tables. Prose and a numbered list.

## Treatments

- The overachievers: the classic countdown from `candidates` — who is getting
  the most from the least, one case per coach. (If recent coverage already
  ran an overachievement piece, the rotation should have moved you off this
  treatment; trust the brief.)
- The underachievers: written from `strugglers`. The biggest jobs in the
  sport measured against what their history demands — who is winning least
  with the most, what the program's own trophy case says the standard is, and
  which of these seasons reads as a blip versus a slide. No hot-seat
  speculation and no job-security talk; this is about the work, not the
  employment.
- Program futures: rank the next three years, not the season. Blend both
  lists: a struggler with a young coach and a proud history may outrank an
  overachiever whose ceiling is his conference. Argue from tenure, trajectory
  and what each program has historically been able to demand.
- The five-year forecast: which of these coaches holds a bigger job in five
  years, which has found his level, and which struggler's program is in a
  slide its history will not tolerate. Trajectories (`program.recentSeasons`),
  not this season's table — and still no talk of anyone's employment.
- The hires board: structure the piece as four head-to-head decisions — two
  coaches at a time, from either list, which one you hire today with the
  same roster and why. The comparisons carry the ranking implicitly.
