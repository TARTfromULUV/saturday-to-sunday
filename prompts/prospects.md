# The young players to know

The freshmen and sophomores already producing like upperclassmen, and who are
going to be a problem for somebody for two more years.

You are given `players`, ranked: each one's name, position, class year,
team, team rank, conference, what he was `recruitedAs` out of high school,
`arrived` when he is in his first season at the school after a transfer or a
junior college, and his `season` production line. The order is this site's,
by production relative to what is normal at his position, so No. 1 is the
underclassman furthest ahead of his class, whatever position he plays.
`freshmanCount` says how many of them are true freshmen.

450 to 700 words.

## The open

Two paragraphs, no subhead. Say what this list is and — more importantly —
what got someone on it: production, now, in games that matter, at an age when
most players are waiting their turn. Name the No. 1 player in the open as the
example, with the number that put him there.

Then one line handing off to the list.

## The list

In the order given, a numbered list. Every entry opens the same way and then
argues:

```
1. **Deuce Whitfield**, RB, Fr., No. 12 Boise State: [two to four sentences]
```

Rank in front of the school when the school is ranked. Then, in the sentences:

- **What he has actually done.** The production line, used as evidence rather
  than recited. "417 yards on 61 carries" is a fact; "417 yards for a team
  ranked twelfth" is a sentence.
- **What it means at his age.** A freshman doing this is a different story
  from a sophomore, and `arrived` changes it again: a transfer in his first
  season here earned the job somewhere else first.
- **What his recruiting stars said.** `recruitedAs` is a published fact. A
  five-star living up to it is a short entry; a three-star outproducing the
  five-stars above him on the depth charts of the country is the reason
  anyone reads this.

Vary the shape of the entries. Twelve paragraphs that each start with a name
and a stat line is a table with sentences in it.

## What makes an entry good

The gap between what somebody was supposed to be and what he is, and the gap
between his age and his numbers. Where a team's situation explains the player
— a ranked team leaning on a freshman — say it from the rank and the line,
and nothing more: no depth chart, no injury to the man ahead of him, no
"took the job in October". None of that is given.

## Rules

- Production numbers must come from each player's `season` line. Never invent
  a stat, a snap count, a game, or a depth chart.
- Nothing about how anyone is graded, rated or projected to develop. No
  ratings exist in what you are given and none are published. The only
  evaluations here are the stars he was recruited with and the numbers he has
  produced.
- Star ratings and `arrived` are published facts and may be used freely.
- These are underclassmen. Do not write about their draft stock, do not give
  them a round grade, and do not say anyone is leaving early.
- Do not compare a player to a real professional athlete by name.
- No quotes in this piece.

## Treatments

- The ranking: the default — the list in order, the No. 1 in the open, the
  shape of each entry varied.
- (requires: freshmanCount) The freshmen first: pull the true freshmen to
  the top (`year`), argue each one as the rarest thing in the sport, then the
  sophomores as the players who did this a year ago and are still doing it.
- Five names, five cases: pick only the five most compelling players and give
  each a section of his own. For each, make the argument a rival coordinator
  would make: this is the one you game-plan around, and here is why.
- The stars were wrong: order the list by the gap between `recruitedAs` and
  the production, biggest gap first — the three-stars at the top, the
  five-stars doing what five-stars do at the bottom — and say what the
  recruiting rankings missed, in terms of what each one is actually doing.
- Ranked teams first: group by whether the player's team is ranked
  (`teamRank`). The players carrying ranked teams at nineteen are the first
  half; the ones producing for unranked teams, where the opportunity was
  bigger and the games smaller, are the second. Say which is the harder
  thing to do.
