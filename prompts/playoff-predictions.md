# Playoff predictions

The Playoff desk before the committee exists. There are no CFP rankings yet
and no field to report — this piece is the staff going on the record about
what the field WILL be, which is a better read than a bulletin about a
bracket nobody has seeded. The treatment in your brief decides which
prediction this week's piece makes; commit to it.

550 to 800 words.

## The one honesty rule that is different here

The committee has not published anything. Never cite "the committee", a CFP
ranking, a seed line, or "the current bracket" — none of those exist yet.
The media poll (`pollTop`), records, conference standings and remaining
schedules are your evidence. Predictions are yours to make; evidence is not.

## What you have

- `pollTop` — the media poll, top 16, with records.
- `contenders` — the ranked teams with conference record, standing, and
  every remaining game (opponent, rank, home or away). The remaining
  schedule is the argument: a contender is a résumé PLUS the games that can
  still wreck it.
- `conferenceFrontRunners` — who currently leads each league. Champions take
  automatic bids, so a prediction about the field is partly a prediction
  about these races.
- `preseason` true means zero games have been played — say so, enjoy it, and
  be appropriately reckless. By midseason the calls should be tighter and
  the piece should say what has already been proven wrong.
- `champion` is `required` on the preseason edition: whatever the treatment,
  the piece names the staff's national champion, in the headline or the
  first paragraph, and names the full twelve-team field somewhere in it.
  That call is the one readers will hold the desk to in January.

## Rules

- Make actual calls. "Could go either way" is not a prediction. Name teams,
  name the miss, name the champion, and give each call its one best reason.
- Argue from results, schedule and conference position — never from a
  rating, a prestige value, or anything that sounds like a game menu.
- No invented odds or percentages. "The schedule says otherwise" is
  analysis; "a 73% chance" is fabrication.
- The desk will be held to these calls in print later in the season, and the
  piece should own that plainly — one line is enough.
- No tables. Prose, and one numbered or dashed list where the treatment
  calls for the field or the picks.

## Treatments

- The field, called now: the staff's twelve, named outright — champions and
  at-larges under the format's rules (conference champions take the
  automatic bids; use `conferenceFrontRunners`). One line of reasoning per
  team is plenty; spend the prose on the two or three calls that would get
  this desk shouted at.
- The champion case: three or four teams that can actually win the whole
  thing, one argued case each — what carries them through four rounds in
  January, and the one game left on the schedule (`remaining`) that tests
  the case. End by picking ONE.
- The dark horses: teams ranked tenth or worse (or leading a league nobody
  respects) who end up in the field. Argue each from the schedule — who
  they still get at home, whose collapse opens the door.
- The first out: the highly ranked team that misses. The schedule is the
  weapon here — walk the remaining games and show where the second loss
  comes from, and who takes the spot instead.
- The conference calls: pick every league's champion, then say what those
  picks do to the field — which league sends two or three, and which
  front-runner (`conferenceFrontRunners`) is holding a lead the staff does
  not believe in.
