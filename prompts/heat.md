# The coaching market

Two halves of one story: who is in trouble, and who the schools in trouble are
going to call. A piece that only lists men about to be fired is half the story
and the mean half. Run them together.

You are NOT given a job-security number, and you must not invent one. There is
no percentage, no index, no rating. What you get is evidence: records,
conference standing, current rank, streaks, contract years remaining and what
the contract asks for, what the athletic director prioritises, how last week
went, and `program` — what each school has actually won, all time, plus
`program.recentSeasons`, the last few seasons' records, finishes, final
ranks and the coach who had the job each year.

Three words describe a seat without a number and are the only ones you have:
`pressure` (`critical`, `elevated`, `steady`, `settled`) is how hot it is now;
`standingAtSeasonStart` and `standingNow` are the school's own reading of the
job in August and today, so "safe in August, on the hot seat by November" is a
fact. `trend` says whether the seat moved this week. Use them as emphasis, not
as evidence: the evidence is the record beside them.

500 to 800 words.

## The open

Two paragraphs, unsigned, naming the shape of the market right now. Is this a
year with real jobs opening, or a quiet one? Is there one obvious pairing —
a school that is going to need a coach and a coach who is obviously ready for
a school like it? Say it here, then let the sections prove it.

## `## Under pressure`

The coaches in `underPressure`, and `worsenedThisWeek` for anyone whose
situation changed this week. Three or four given real paragraphs, not a list.
Each one opens with the coach and school in bold, then the case:

- **Lead with what changed, not with the record.** Who they lost to, by how
  much, at home or away (`lastResult`, when there is one; a coach with none
  was idle, and nothing happened to him this week). `fellOutOfPoll`,
  `lostToUnranked` and `conferenceStanding` are the strongest evidence you
  have.
- **`program` is what makes a record mean something.** Thirty conference
  titles and a 4-5 start is a crisis; the same record at a school with none is
  a good year. One number from it, never a list, and never a preseason rank.
  `recentSeasons` says whether this is a slide or a blip: three straight
  losing years under the same man is the story, one bad October is not.
- **`career` is his, not the school's.** `timesFired`, titles won elsewhere, a
  career record — fair context, in a clause.
- **Contract years remaining change the situation.** A coach with one year left
  is in a different position from one signed through the end of the decade at
  the same record.
- **The athletic director sets the temperature.** An impatient AD and a patient
  one produce different stories from identical records.

## `## Names to watch`

The coaches in `onTheRise` — men winning at places that may not be able to keep
them. Three to six of them, each a short paragraph opening the same way. **If
`onTheRise` is empty, this section does not exist**: close the piece after the
pressure section instead, and never name a coach who is not in the data.

```
**Marcus Hale, Tulane:** [three or four sentences]
```

What each entry has to do:

- **The record, and what it is worth.** A 10-2 season means one thing in a
  league the team is dominating and another in one it shares.
  `conferenceRecord`, `streak` and the national offensive and defensive ranks
  are how you show a team is actually good rather than merely winning.
- **Why he might move, or why he might not.** `program` (the school's whole
  history) against his own `careerRecord` and `careerConferenceTitles` is the
  whole argument: a coach with two conference titles at a school that had
  never won one has done the job there. Tenure cuts both ways — a man in year
  six at his alma mater is a different proposition from one in year two.
- **What kind of job fits.** In general terms only: a bigger league, a program
  with money, a school that wants his side of the ball. `specialty` and
  `almaMater` are fair to use when they point somewhere.

Never say a coach has been contacted, is a finalist, is interviewing, or has
been offered anything. Nothing has happened. You are reading a market, not
reporting a search.

## Tone and rules

- Never say "hot seat index", "job security", "percentage", or "rating".
- Do not report a firing or a hire as decided or imminent. Describe the
  pressure and let the reader conclude.
- Do not invent a coach's age, salary, buyout, or contract value. None of that
  is in front of you.
- No unnamed sources in this piece. That exception belongs to the money beat
  and nowhere else. Every claim here is drawn from the record.
- These are people's jobs. Be direct about the results without being gleeful.
- Do not write a table. This is a column, not a leaderboard.
- No quotes.

## Treatments

- The ledger: the default two halves — who is in trouble, then the names the
  schools in trouble will call.
- (requires: onTheRise) The other side of the desk: write it from the
  athletic directors' chairs. Which programs are quietly preparing to move,
  what each AD's stated priority says the search would chase, and which names
  in `onTheRise` fit. Same data, buyer's point of view.
- (requires: onTheRise) The domino: pick the single most likely opening in
  the data and trace the chain it would set off — who from `onTheRise` gets
  the call, and which program ends up the loser of the cycle. The job he
  would leave stays open on the page: nobody outside the data backfills it.
- (requires: underPressure) The slide: order the pressure section by how far
  each program has fallen from its own recent past — `recentSeasons` against
  this year — and separate the men presiding over a decline from the ones
  having one bad season at a school that was already down.
- Contract math: order the piece by contract years remaining. A coach with
  one year left and a bad record is a different story from the same record
  with five years of runway, and the piece is about that difference.
- The case for patience: take one embattled coach and give both arguments
  their full weight — the case the boosters make for a change, the case the
  record and history make for holding. End with a verdict. The rest of the
  list gets one line each.
